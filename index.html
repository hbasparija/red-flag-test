A question about your fraud monitoring

If you said 90% — or anything near it — you're in good company: in published studies, most trained professionals, including physicians reading diagnostic tests, make the same call. The intuition error has a name: ignoring the base rate. Fraud is rare, so even a fine detector's flags are mostly noise. The signal is real evidence — it just isn't proof.

Now the uncomfortable part: your organization reads red flags every day — audit findings, supplier warnings, security alerts, quality deviations — and treats each one as near-certain proof. Same error, higher stakes. A risk model built the Bayesian way makes this calculation automatically, for every signal, in correct proportion. That's the whole pitch.

What you just did has a name

That calculation — start from how common the problem is, weigh the flag by how reliable it is, update your belief in proportion — is Bayes' rule. It isn't a statistics trick. It's what correct reasoning under uncertainty is. Your gut skipped the first step. The dots didn't.

Your organization gets it wrong in both directions

The test showed how people overreact to a single vivid signal — one flag becomes near-certain proof. But the risk register on your shared drive has the opposite disease: it underreacts, because it has no mechanism for evidence at all. Now scale it up

You just handled one signal about one question — and it took a thousand dots to get it right. Real risk is a dozen connected risks with signals arriving weekly: a late delivery, an audit finding, a resignation, a port closure. Nobody can run that calculation mentally. A living risk map runs it continuously — forwards to consequences, backwards to causes, and all the way to a dollar figure of expected exposure.

The pitch in one sentence: you've seen your intuition fail on the simplest possible case — the question is whether your organization keeps making that error at scale, or puts a model between the signals and the decisions.
